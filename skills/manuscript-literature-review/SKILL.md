# Manuscript Literature Review

Write prose the author can put into a manuscript. Use the author's contribution to choose the comparisons. Let the evidence determine what distinction, if any, survives those comparisons.

## Start with the author

Use the language of the conversation for intake. Reuse information already supplied. Ask the unanswered items together, in one short message.

- What is the working title?
- What does the paper contribute? One to three sentences are enough. If status is unclear and matters, ask which components are proposed, implemented, or evaluated.
- Which citation and reference style should I use? The default is APA 7th edition.

Honor an explicit journal style, output language, source restriction, or length. Default to APA 7. Include the matrix in the manuscript unless the author requests a supplement or explicitly opts out. The author's title and description of the contribution are enough to start. An abstract, journal name, and seed papers can help when available. Obtain the contribution before drafting a positioning claim.

## Find the evidence

Follow the author's source restrictions first. If the author requests a review based only on supplied works, use that corpus and verify what the supplied evidence supports. Apply the external discovery steps when their scope permits it.

1. **Route discovery.** Inspect the available tools, including deferred connectors. When Consensus is installed and usable, actually run its scholarly search. Fetch each selected record before citing it and inspect accessible full text where the claim needs it. Respect current connector requirements for attribution. If numbered source markers are required when reporting Consensus results, retain their mapping in a separate discovery note and use the author's chosen citation style in the manuscript.
2. **Handle availability.** If Consensus is absent, offer installation once, preferably alongside intake. Use the host's plugin suggestion flow if available. If the author declines or has already chosen search, proceed with academic and general web search. If the connector is present but returns an access, quota, or service error, use the available search routes and identify the actual limitation. If the user accepted installation, check for a callable tool after setup. A failed setup need not interrupt the review.
3. **Build a corpus beyond the first page.** Search separately for the problem, method or theory, and contribution. Run distinct searches for origins, methodological branches, recent work, and close precedents. When Consensus is usable, distribute these queries across multiple Consensus searches and supplement them with at least one independent academic or primary-source search route when available. A batch of 10 hits is one discovery pass. Use alternate terms, date windows, subject indexes, scholarly web search, and backward and forward citation links to find additional studies. Open publisher, proceedings, archive, or institutional records for verification. Treat search snippets as leads.
4. **Recover the first generation of work.** Identify the field's earliest verifiable definitions, formulations, algorithms, experimental demonstrations, or foundational results, as applicable. Start from multiple scholarly lineages where terminology or priority is contested. Trace references backward from reviews and close studies, then run author, historical-term, and early-date searches. Consult original articles or primary foundational publications and trace the intervening work that altered their assumptions, measurement, methods, or scope. Distinguish documented priority, often-cited popularization, and subsequent refinements. When original texts are inaccessible, identify what you verified and attribute any secondary account or indirect quotation accordingly.
5. **Search through the present.** Run a date-bounded query through the search date, including current-year and online-first work. Start with the last 24 months and adjust to the field's pace. Follow forward citations from key papers where possible. Seek recent direct precedents, null findings, and replications under alternative terminology. Prefer the version of record when a preprint has since been published. Label unpublished preprints explicitly and report the recent window and any gaps.
6. **Test the proposed contribution.** Search for each component independently and for their combination. Seek studies with overlapping mechanisms or objectives under different names. Compare the strongest competing studies in terms of question, setting, method, evaluation, and result as applicable. If a predecessor already achieves the claimed advance, acknowledge it and narrow the distinction to what the author can substantiate. If the evidence supports a replication, extension, or new context, describe that contribution on its own terms.
7. **Reach the review minimum.** For a full review, examine at least **20 distinct relevant prior scholarly works** with evidence sufficient to assess a relevant claim. Treat 20 as the minimum and continue where major themes, foundational work, late developments, or close precedents remain thin. A paper counts when you verify its identity, inspect at least its abstract or an equivalent substantive primary record, and log what it contributes or how it compares. Full text or supplements are required for details the abstract cannot substantiate. Deduplicate preprints, proceedings extensions reporting the same evidence, and versions of record at the level of the underlying work. Count substantive extensions as distinct only when their new evidence or results justify it. Exclude the present manuscript, metadata-only records, duplicate versions, and irrelevant hits. Carry the reviewed works into the prose or matrix through substantive comparisons or synthesis and cite them. An uncited reading list or citation cluster assembled to meet the count cannot substitute for coverage.

If the evidence-supported count is below 20, refine historical and recent queries, follow citations, and check at least one additional permitted route before finalizing when external discovery is allowed. If the field is sparse, the author restricts the corpus, or access limits prevent reaching 20, deliver the strongest review possible and report the actual count, searches tried, and specific reason for the shortfall.

Keep a working record of the search date, channels and queries used, inclusion scope, and coverage limits. For each counted work, record verified identity, version, evidence access, its role in the synthesis, and a locator for consequential claims and matrix decisions. Track overlapping datasets or reports before describing findings as independent. Cite original evidence for specific results and reviews for field-level synthesis. Check correction and retraction notices where relevant. Qualify details beyond the accessible text.

## Write the section

For a full standalone review with no stated limit, target **3,000–4,500 words of manuscript prose**, excluding the matrix, references, and search note. Use the author's or venue's length limit when provided. When a narrow or restricted corpus cannot support that depth, write to the depth the evidence supports and explain the coverage limit in the search note. Develop substantive subsections on origins and evolution, methodological or theoretical streams, the current evidence, and the closest comparisons. Give each major stream enough space to explain its assumptions, evidence, findings, and unresolved questions. Use these seven writing tasks as rhetorical moves. Headings and order should suit the manuscript.

| Task | What the reader should learn |
|---|---|
| Frame the relevant literature | Define the problem and its scope. Introduce the distinction that organizes the studies and explain its relevance to the paper's question. |
| Give a historical perspective | Connect verified turning points. Explain which earlier constraint or assumption each advance addressed. |
| Compare methodological approaches | Group studies by how they answer the question. Show how a consequential design choice changes the interpretation or limits comparison. |
| State what is currently known | Distinguish converging results, live disagreements, and underexamined conditions. Weight conclusions by study design, independence, and relevance. |
| Describe published work precisely | Name the researcher or study when attribution matters. Use verbs that fit the evidence, such as *proposed*, *observed*, *estimated*, or *replicated*. Put the citation beside the claim. |
| Introduce quotations selectively | Quote when exact language matters, such as a disputed definition. Introduce it with a signal phrase, verify the wording in the original, give a page or other valid locator, and explain the quoted distinction. Otherwise paraphrase. |
| Synthesize and position | Close major themes with the inference they support. End with the closest comparison, what remains unresolved within the reviewed scope, and the specific contribution the author describes. |

Build paragraphs around a shared question. For example, if two studies disagree, examine whether they used the same outcome, follow-up interval, and population before suggesting an explanation. Distinguish a demonstrated explanation from a plausible one. Avoid treating a difference in statistical significance as evidence that the two estimates differ. For conceptual work, compare definitions, assumptions, and scope conditions. For computational work, attend to datasets, baselines, and evaluation protocols.

## Deliver and verify

Return the manuscript section in the requested language, the detailed [positioning matrix](references/positioning-matrix.md), and a complete reference list. Read the matrix guide before building the comparison. Check that the matrix is included unless the author explicitly opted out. If the author requests a supplement, provide it with the review. Keep tool messages and search process out of the manuscript and give the author a short separate note stating the search cutoff, channels used, distinct works reviewed, and material coverage or verification limits. If the count falls below 20, give the actual count and reason in that note.

Apply the chosen style to both in-text citations and references. In APA 7, use author–date citations, disambiguate same-author/same-year works, alphabetize the list, and present verified DOIs as DOI URLs. Use the appropriate reference format for the actual publication type. Reconcile citations in both prose and table with the reference list in both directions. Check names, dates, titles, venues, publication details and identifiers against the publisher or a reliable scholarly record. Supply only verified fields. Revisit the evidence behind quotations, numerical claims, novelty statements, and table cells. Describe proposed work in prospective language until the author supplies evidence of completion.
