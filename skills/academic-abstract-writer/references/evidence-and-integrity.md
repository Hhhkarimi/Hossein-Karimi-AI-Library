# Evidence And Integrity

## Establish the evidence before writing

Identify the research type, completion status, and intended audience. Read the available substantive sections, not just the existing abstract. Internally extract an evidence map:

- Research context, specific problem, and documented gap or motivation.
- Primary objective, question, hypothesis, or central thesis.
- Study design, data source, population/sample, setting, intervention or comparator, analytical method, or theoretical approach, as relevant.
- Primary outcomes, key quantitative estimates, qualitative themes, formal results, or central arguments.
- Direction, magnitude, units, uncertainty, and evaluation conditions where relevant.
- Supported interpretation, contribution, implications, and material limitations.
- Journal requirements, registration, or funding details when required and supplied.

Anchor every substantive statement to the manuscript or explicitly supplied research information. Verify numerical claims against the source results and relevant tables. Distinguish original findings from cited background literature, hypotheses, proposed methods, and discussion speculation. Do not silently resolve consequential inconsistencies between tables, results, and an existing abstract. If an inconsistency cannot be resolved from the source, omit the disputed detail if nonessential; otherwise ask a focused question or flag it outside a clearly labeled provisional abstract.

Never fabricate or infer unsupported sample sizes, datasets, statistical estimates, p-values, confidence intervals, effect sizes, baselines, dates, ethics approvals, registrations, novelty, mechanisms, or findings. Do not infer that an unreported outcome is negative, null, or statistically insignificant. Do not fill gaps from general knowledge or external literature as if they were the study's results.

## Enforce scientific and stylistic quality

- Use precise and consistent terms, concrete verbs, economical syntax, and natural sentence variation. Prefer clarity to inflated formality. Active or passive voice is acceptable when it improves comprehension.
- Preserve appropriate tense and modality in the output language. Describe completed methods and observations as completed, distinguish current knowledge from new findings, and state planned work in future or prospective terms.
- Keep the abstract self-contained. Define essential unfamiliar acronyms on first use when space and venue rules allow. Avoid unnecessary abbreviations, footnotes, citations, equations, and references to tables or figures; include them only where scientifically necessary or explicitly required.
- Report central null, negative, unexpected, and inconclusive findings honestly. Do not selectively foreground secondary positive outcomes while hiding the primary result.
- Do not equate statistical significance with scientific or practical importance, non-significance with equivalence, correlation with causation, or benchmark performance with universal superiority.
- Calibrate certainty and generalizability to study design and evidence. Distinguish association, prediction, explanation, causal inference, and speculation. Preserve population, setting, time, and evaluation boundaries when material.
- State novelty through the actual supported contribution, not unverified priority claims such as 'first ever', 'groundbreaking', or 'unprecedented'. Avoid promotional adjectives, exaggerated impact, vague filler, and repetitive phrasing.
- Do not claim results not present in the source, change their direction, or manipulate numerical precision to make findings appear stronger. Do not disguise plagiarism or evade AI-detection systems. Write original prose that faithfully summarizes the source.
- Do not insert discussion of the editing process, the model, or keyword optimization into the abstract itself.

## Handle missing, incomplete, or contradictory input

- No article or substantive research content: request the manuscript or the minimum information needed (objective, approach, and main results or argument).
- Sufficient content: proceed without unnecessary clarification, even if the journal or preferred length is unspecified.
- Incomplete source with enough verified content: provide a conservative abstract using only the available information. If missing material prevents a complete research abstract, label it provisional outside the paragraph and briefly identify the missing elements.
- Completed empirical study with no supplied results: request principal results; if the user explicitly wants a provisional draft, provide a truthful purpose-and-method abstract without invented results and identify its status separately.
- Planned study or protocol: provide an appropriate prospective abstract, not a fabricated completed-study abstract.
- Inaccessible or unreadable file: state what could not be read and request readable text or the essential missing sections. Do not pretend to have inspected unavailable content.
- Multiple manuscripts: handle each separately; never merge unrelated studies unless explicitly asked to synthesize them.
